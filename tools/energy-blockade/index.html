      Run summary

      How to read it

      Taiwan imports about 97 percent of its energy by sea. A blockade does not switch the lights off on day one. It starts a set of clocks, one per fuel, and each clock runs at a different speed. Liquefied natural gas, which generated almost half of Taiwan's electricity in 2025, is stored in tanks that hold about eleven days of normal use. Coal yards hold roughly six weeks. Oil stocks last months, but almost none of that oil makes electricity.

      Set how much inbound cargo the blockade stops and how long it lasts, then press Play or drag the day slider. The four tanks show what is left of each fuel and how much of normal demand the grid can meet. The chart shows where the power comes from each day. The strip below it shows which parts of the economy keep power, are rationed or go dark, in the order your rationing policy sets.

      Three things usually decide the outcome: the LNG cliff in the second week, the coal cliff around week six or seven, and how much demand Taiwan is willing to shed before either one arrives. Try a full blockade with and without demand reduction and compare the day the grid falls below half.

      How the model works

      Each fuel is a stock measured in days of normal consumption. Every day, arrivals equal normal supply times the share of cargo that gets through, and the grid burns fuel to meet demand. Renewables run at their normal share. Coal is dispatched first to stretch LNG, then gas, then oil. Power stations get fuel before other users once a stock is empty, as in the CSIS wargame model. When supply falls short, power goes to sectors in the priority order you pick. After the blockade lifts, imports return with some spare capacity to refill stocks.

      Stock levels, the generation mix and the fuel shares used for power are sourced. The blockade percentages, sector shares of demand, the priority orders, the size of each demand-reduction measure, the restock rate and the backup-generation figure are notional round numbers chosen to show structure. The model ignores weather, LNG boil-off, grid stability limits, strikes on terminals or plants, ship-by-ship arrival timing, restart delays at power plants after the blockade lifts, and emergency imports by air. Use it to reason about which clock runs out first and what buys time, not to predict a date.
