Click a chokepoint marker to close or reopen it. Lanes are hand-drawn and approximate; real ships follow traffic schemes, weather and draft limits the model leaves out.

How to read it

Each voyage follows the shortest path through a network of approximate sea lanes. Close a chokepoint and the path jumps to the next-shortest way around. The gray dashed line keeps the all-open route on the map for comparison, and the tiles in the panel show what the detour adds: nautical miles, days at the speed you set, and a cost for one ship at a daily rate you set.

The cost is a framing device. It multiplies extra days by one daily number that stands in for charter, fuel and crew, so it is marked notional. Real costs depend on ship type, market rates, insurance and how many ships reroute at once. For a sourced order of magnitude, CSIS China Power estimated that a week-long closure of the Strait of Malacca would add about $64.5 million in shipping costs across all affected ships, assuming tankers and bulk carriers over 100,000 deadweight tons divert through Lombok and smaller ships through Sunda.

The Taiwan Strait contingency

A blockade, quarantine or invasion of Taiwan would directly touch the Taiwan Strait, the Luzon Strait and Bashi Channel, and the waters east of Taiwan toward Yonaguni. CSIS notes that ships bound for China could reroute through the Miyako Strait, where they would still be exposed. Kaohsiung and Taichung sit on the Strait itself, so for Taiwan's own ports there is no detour. More Taiwan Security Monitor research is at tsm.schar.gmu.edu.

Method and sources

  • Oil flows: U.S. Energy Information Administration, World Oil Transit Chokepoints (updated March 3, 2026). Hormuz and Malacca widths: the 2017 edition of the same EIA report.
  • Trade values, reroute order and the Taiwan contingency: Hart, Funaiole, Peng, Verschuur, Lin and Li, Troubled Straits: Analyzing Trade Chokepoints in the South China Sea, CSIS, July 1, 2026. Week-long Malacca closure cost: CSIS China Power, How Much Trade Transits the South China Sea?
  • Widths and depths not given by EIA or CSIS come from the Wikipedia articles on the Malacca, Sunda, Lombok, Luzon, Taiwan, Miyako and Korea straits. Japan's 3-mile limit in the Tsushima Strait is checked against the text of its 1977 Law on the Territorial Sea (UN DOALOS). Makassar, Luzon Strait, Bashi and Taiwan–Yonaguni widths were measured for this tool on Natural Earth land and are labeled "measured".
  • Sea lanes: about 110 hand-placed waypoints joined by great-circle segments, checked so that no segment crosses Natural Earth 1:50m land (scripts/lanes.py). Routes are the shortest path through that network (Dijkstra). They are approximate, so the absolute distances are less reliable than the differences between routes.
  • The Strait of Hormuz is shown for context and cannot be closed here. The "extra" toggles (waters east of Taiwan, eastern Indonesian passages) are not among the eight named chokepoints; they exist so the reroutes have somewhere honest to go. With every Indonesian passage closed, ships go around Australia, the worst case CSIS describes.
  • Speed (default 14 knots) and daily ship cost (default $60,000) are notional inputs you control.
  • Map: Natural Earth 1:50m land (public domain).
