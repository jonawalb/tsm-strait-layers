Reported cable damage around Taiwan

Click a date or a card. Replays set the network to that year and cut the cables named in reporting.

How to read this

Almost all of Taiwan's international internet and phone traffic travels through submarine fiber-optic cables. This map shows every cable that TeleGeography's public Submarine Cable Map lists as landing in Taiwan ( in service or under construction, plus planned systems if you switch them on), where each comes ashore, and the domestic cables that link the main island to Penghu, Kinmen and the Matsu islands.

The simulator treats the network as a graph. Each island is a node, and landing points on the same island are assumed to be joined over land. Cutting an international cable at a Taiwanese landing removes that landing's link to the outside world; cutting a domestic segment removes one link between islands. The panel then reports, for each island, whether it can still reach a foreign landing, whether its only remaining route runs through a cable that lands solely in mainland China, and how many further cuts would isolate it.

The count is of cables, not capacity. TeleGeography's public data does not include bandwidth, and a new trans-Pacific system carries far more traffic than a 2001 regional one. Two cables left standing can mean very different things. The model also leaves out satellite links, microwave radio backups and the time it takes to reroute traffic, so "cut off" here means cut off by cable. Matsu, for one, has a microwave backup (reported in 2023 as a Yangmingshan–Dongyin link) with a maximum capacity of 12.6 Gbps by January 2025, against a weekday peak load of about 9.5 Gbps, and satellite dishes as a further backup (Taipei Times, 2023; 2025).

Official counts differ from the public map. In May 2024 the head of Taiwan's National Security Bureau told legislators that Taiwan has 14 international cables and 10 cables to its outlying islands (Taipei Times). The Ministry of Digital Affairs counted 15 international and 10 domestic cables as of the end of February 2026 (MODA, April 2026). TeleGeography lists only three domestic systems, TPKM2, TPKM3 and Taiwan–Matsu No. 4, so some of the domestic links are missing from this model, which makes the outlying islands look more fragile than they are.

Cables break often, from fishing gear, anchors and wear. The timeline under the map lists incidents around Taiwan that were covered in reliable reporting, with the vessels named where reporting named them. Taiwan Security Monitor is also researching cable landing stations; this tool covers only what the public cable data and the cited reporting show.

Method and caveats

  • Cable routes are schematic. TeleGeography draws them to show which places a cable joins, not the exact path on the seabed.
  • Landing points are kept at the town level TeleGeography publishes. The tool shows no station addresses or other infrastructure.
  • International cables that land more than once in Taiwan (EAC-C2C, SJC2, AUG East) can be cut landing by landing. Domestic cables are split into landing-to-landing segments derived from TeleGeography's route geometry; the split is ours.
  • "Cuts to isolate" is the number of edge-disjoint cable routes from an island to any foreign landing (a max-flow count with every cable link worth one).
  • The year slider uses each cable's ready-for-service year. Cables retired before today are not in TeleGeography's current data, so earlier years undercount the network.
  • Historical replays match reported cable names to TeleGeography's records. Reporting on the 2023 Matsu incident names the "Taiwan–Matsu No. 2" and "No. 3" cables; TeleGeography lists the Matsu legs of TPKM2 and TPKM3. Treating them as the same cables is our inference; it fits later reporting that places damage to "No. 3" on the Dongyin–Beigan section and to "No. 2" near the Dongyin cable station, which is where TeleGeography's TPKM3 and TPKM2 run.
  • Replays cut only the cables named in reporting and do not model repair times. The December 2025 earthquake damage has no replay: reporting names the cables but not the damaged sections, and cutting whole cables would overstate it.
  • Taiwan Security Monitor's cable incident database (September 2026) lists a February 2024 Matsu No. 3 incident. Its cited source (Taipei Times, August 5, 2024) describes the February 8, 2023 cut already shown here, so the timeline does not list it twice. Where that database and the cited reporting differ on a cable name or date, the timeline follows the reporting.
